# Overnight thermal recovery and sustained loading

An oil-immersed transformer responds to a change in ambient temperature with a lag of several hours, set by the thermal mass of the oil and core. This lag is what makes consecutive warm nights more damaging than a single hot afternoon.

## The recovery mechanism

Under normal conditions the unit sheds accumulated heat overnight and begins the following day at close to its starting temperature. The overnight minimum determines how much heat is shed, and the thermal time constant determines how much of the available recovery is realised.

Where the overnight minimum remains above roughly 24 degrees, recovery is incomplete. The unit begins the next day warmer than it began the previous one, and the starting point ratchets upward across the event. By the third or fourth day, hot-spot temperature can exceed that reached on a shorter event with a materially higher peak ambient.

## Implications for assessment

Assessing exposure on peak ambient temperature alone will rank a short severe event above a long moderate one, which inverts the actual thermal loading. Assessment should account for:

- The number of consecutive nights with a minimum above the recovery threshold
- The accumulated hours above the reference ambient, not only the maximum reached
- The duration of the event

## Operational response

On events forecast to run beyond 48 hours with warm overnight minima, the loading reduction in MG-021 applies from the third day. Where forced cooling is available, run it continuously through the overnight period rather than allowing the thermostat to drop the stages out; the recovery gained overnight is worth more than the fan running cost.

Units with high thermal mass recover more slowly and should be prioritised accordingly on sustained events.
